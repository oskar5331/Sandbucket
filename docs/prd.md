# Product Requirements Document
## Sandbucket — Personal & Small Business Accounting

**Version:** 1.0 (MVP)
**Status:** Final — approved for build, 2026-11-01
**Audience:** Technical — developers building from scratch

---

## 1. Vision

A free, open-source, mobile-first bookkeeping tool built for small businesses. Anyone can self-host it or use the hosted version. Each user runs one or more independent bookkeeping profiles — mixing personal and business use in the same account is intentional. No accountant required to get started.

---

## 2. Problem Statement

Small business owners and freelancers need to track cash flow, invoice payments, and expenses. Existing tools (Merit Aktiva, Directo, Xero) are either too expensive, too complex, or designed for larger teams. Spreadsheets are common but fragile and unstructured. There is no lightweight, mobile-friendly, free option that applies correct bookkeeping fundamentals while staying simple enough for a non-accountant to use on their phone.

---

## 3. Target Users

### Primary — MVP
**Micro-business owner / freelancer (Estonian market)**
- Sole trader or OÜ (private limited company) with < 10 employees
- Needs to track cash flow: expenses, income, invoices, bank movements, and small investments
- Files VAT or income tax returns quarterly/annually via e-MTA
- Works mostly from phone; rarely sits at a desktop
- Not an accountant; understands income and expenses but not double-entry bookkeeping

### Secondary — MVP
**Household bookkeeper**
- A family member tracking shared income and expenses alongside or within the same account as their business
- Does not need invoices or VAT; just clean categorised records
- Profile separation means business and personal books stay isolated even within one account

### Out of scope for MVP
- Accountants managing multiple client companies
- Businesses with employees (payroll)
- VAT-registered businesses requiring VAT-line itemisation per transaction
- Companies requiring integration with e-MTA or banking APIs

## 4. Core Concepts

The data model follows double-entry bookkeeping. Users do not need to understand this — the UI abstracts it entirely — but the underlying engine must enforce it correctly.

### Party
Any entity that money moves to or from. Includes the user's own bank accounts, cash registers, suppliers, customers, and internal accounting accounts (Profits, Expenses). Every transaction involves exactly two parties: one that gives (credit) and one that receives (debit).

Each Party has a type. Types are either **non-assignable** (purely ledger labels — bank, cash register, profits, expenses, investment, supplier, customer) or **user-assignable** (partner, worker). A user-assignable Party can be linked to a member of the profile — a real user account (§6.20). The member's tenant role (`owner`, `accountant`, or `worker`) governs what they can do in the profile; the Party type simply declares that the slot for a real person exists. Each member is linked to at most one Party per profile.

### Record
A single double-entry accounting line. Has a **credit party** (source of money), a **debit party** (destination of money), an amount, a date, and a description. Records are never created directly by the user — they are created by the server-side transaction handlers, one per transaction type.

### TransactionGroup
A logical grouping of one or more Records representing a single real-world event. A paid expense produces one Record; a micro-investment produces two. The TransactionGroup is what the user sees and interacts with — the Records are its accounting implementation detail.

### Balance
Each Party maintains a running `CreditBalance` and `DebitBalance`, recalculated from the full set of its Records every time a Record is saved or deleted. Balances are never entered or edited manually.

### Transaction Types
The original seven are supported from day one, plus two added after the legacy business-logic review (`business-logic-spec.md`): **Settlement** (cash-custodian redesign, `cash-custodian.md`) and **Opening Balance** (§25). Nine in total:

| Type | What it represents | Records created |
|---|---|---|
| **Expense** | Paying a supplier invoice | 1 — Money store (Bank or CashRegister) or Partner → Supplier |
| **Income** | Receiving a customer payment | 1 — Customer → Money store (Bank or CashRegister) or Partner |
| **Received Invoice** | Recording a supplier invoice (not yet paid) | 1 — Supplier → Expenses or Investment |
| **Issued Invoice** | Sending an invoice to a customer | 1 — Profits → Customer |
| **Withdrawal** | Taking cash out of the bank | 1 — Bank → CashRegister (optionally a custodian's, §6.19) |
| **Deposit** | Putting cash into the bank | 1 — Partner/Person → Bank |
| **Micro-investment** | Investor funds a cost via a supplier | 2 — Supplier→Investment; Investor→Supplier |
| **Settlement** | A custodian returns unspent cash to the bank | 1 — Custodian's CashRegister → Bank |
| **Opening Balance** | Seeding a party's starting balance when historical books predate the system | 1 — Party↔Opening Balances (direction depends on whether seeding a credit or debit starting position) |

## 5. Goals

### MVP Goals
- A user can sign up, create a profile, and record their first transaction in under 3 minutes on a mobile phone
- All nine transaction types are available from the first release
- Double-entry rules are enforced without the user ever being aware of them
- The app is installable as a PWA on Android and iOS
- The codebase is publicly available under an open-source license
- Hosted on Fly.io (app Machines + Managed Postgres + Tigris object storage); zero infrastructure to manage for the user
- Self-hostable as a fallback for technical users who want full data ownership

### Success Metrics (MVP)
- Time-to-first-record < 3 minutes from sign-up on mobile
- Works on a mid-range Android phone on a 3G connection
- Lighthouse PWA score ≥ 90
- Initial JS bundle < 200 KB (as defined in §8)

## 6. Scope

### In Scope — MVP

#### 6.1 Profiles
A single user account can hold multiple profiles. Each profile is an independent bookkeeping context — its own parties, its own transaction history, its own balances, its own currency. A profile can represent a company, a household, a project, or any arbitrary scope the user chooses. On first login a default profile is created automatically with currency `EUR`. When creating subsequent profiles the user picks a currency (ISO 4217 three-letter code, EUR pre-selected). Currency is fixed once the profile exists — see §10. Users can create, name, and switch between profiles. No data crosses profile boundaries. A profile can be shared with other user accounts, each with a role (§6.20).

#### 6.2 Party Management
Users define the counterparties relevant to their activity. On profile creation the app pre-seeds a set of system parties (Bank, CashRegister, Profits, Expenses, Opening Balances — §6.17) so the user can record the first transaction without any setup. Users can add custom parties, edit existing ones, and deactivate parties they no longer need.

Party types: `Bank`, `CashRegister`, `Supplier`, `Customer`, `Partner`, `Worker`, `Investment`, `Profits`, `Expenses`, and the system-only `OpeningBalances` (seeded once per profile, never user-created, §6.17/§25). A `CashRegister` can optionally name a custodian (§6.19). *(Legacy's `Utility`, `Tenant`, and `Warehouse` enum values are deliberately dropped: `Utility` was only ever a caption convention over `Supplier` — "Utility (Supplier)" — not a distinct bucketing behaviour, so it folds into `Supplier`; `Tenant` and `Warehouse` had no live business-logic role in any traced microflow. See `business-logic-spec.md` §2.4.)*

Parties can optionally be grouped under a parent party for organisational purposes (e.g. grouping several suppliers under one umbrella party). A party can be marked as a group (`is_group`); other parties can reference it as their parent (`parent_party_id`). Purely organisational — no effect on bucketing or balances. See §24.

Parties can be designated as system-default (e.g. "main bank account") so the app can pre-fill them in transaction forms without requiring the user to select them every time.

#### 6.3 Expense Recording
Captures money leaving the business to pay a supplier. The user selects the supplier party, enters the amount, date, and description, and chooses how it was paid: **from a money store** (any Bank or CashRegister party — including a custodian's register, e.g. "Cash held by John", §6.19) or **paid personally by a partner** (the Partner party is credited, so the company now owes them). The two choices are a required field; there is no default fallback branch. Optional receipt attachment (photo or file).

#### 6.4 Income Recording
Captures money arriving from a customer. The user selects the source party, enters the amount, date, description, and chooses where it arrived: **into a money store** (any Bank or CashRegister party) or **received personally by a partner** (the Partner party is debited, so the partner now holds money owed to the company). Required, as for Expense. Optional document attachment.

#### 6.5 Received Invoice
Records a supplier invoice before it is paid. The user enters the supplier, amount, invoice date, description, and classifies the invoice as either a **cost** (routes to the system Expenses party) or an **investment** (routes to a selected Investment party). Optional document attachment. Can later be linked to its payment — see §6.18.

#### 6.6 Issued Invoice
Records an invoice sent to a customer. The user selects the customer party, enters amount, invoice date, and description. The system automatically credits the Profits party. Optional document attachment. Can later be linked to its payment — see §6.18.

#### 6.7 Withdrawal
Records cash or a transfer leaving a bank account into a cash register. The user selects the bank account and the destination CashRegister party — either an unattended register (e.g. office petty cash) or a custodian's register (§6.19). Picking a person who has no register yet creates "Cash held by <name>" with that person as custodian, in the same step. The person's own Partner/Worker party is never debited for cash they hold.

#### 6.8 Deposit
Records cash or a transfer arriving into a bank account from a person or partner. The user selects the source party and the destination bank account.

#### 6.9 Micro-Investment
Records a situation where an investor funds a cost directly. Two Records are created: one from Supplier to Investment account, and one from Investor to Supplier. The user selects the supplier, investment account, investor party, amount, date, and description.

#### 6.10 Transaction List
A paginated list of all transaction groups for the active profile, newest first. Each row shows: date, description, amount, and a transaction type badge. The amount is the one the user entered (a Micro-investment's two Records always carry the same amount). Only real cash movements are signed: **Income** shows `+` in the money-in colour, **Expense** `−` in the money-out colour. Everything else shows the amount unsigned in the muted colour — transfers between the user's own money stores (Withdrawal, Deposit, Settlement), Micro-investment (the investor paid the supplier directly, so no company cash moved), Opening Balance, and both invoice types, which carry an "Invoice" badge so an invoice and its later payment never read as money moving twice. The "Money in" / "Money out" filters therefore contain only Income and Expense and add up to actual cash flow. In the "All" view, an invoice and its payments are shown as one expandable row (§6.18). Searchable by description. Filterable by date range and transaction type. Tapping a row opens the detail view.

#### 6.11 Transaction Detail & Edit
Full view of a transaction group: type, date, amount, description, attached document, and the list of underlying Records (credit party, debit party, amount). Users with edit rights can modify description, date, and amount, or delete the transaction entirely. Rights are role-scoped (§6.20, §32): an `owner` can edit or delete any transaction; an `accountant` only the ones they created; a `worker` only their own, and only until an owner or accountant has edited it. *(Legacy restricts its "advanced edit" — rewiring a Record's credit/debit parties — to SuperAccountant and has a balance-staleness bug on that path; the rebuild doesn't support rewiring at all, per `setup-config.md` §1.2's delete-and-re-enter rule, so that restriction has no analogue here. See `business-logic-spec.md` §6.2, §4.)* Deletion cascades to all child Records and triggers party balance recalculation.

#### 6.12 Party Balance View
A screen per party showing its current credit balance, debit balance, and a chronological list of every Record in which it appears (as credit or debit). Used for checking running balances on a bank account, total paid to a supplier, or total invoiced to a customer.

#### 6.13 Document Attachments
One document can be attached per transaction: either uploaded from the device or captured with the camera. Images are compressed client-side before upload using a WASM MozJPEG encoder (jSquash, §3/§23). The WASM binary is cached by the service worker on first install and loaded from cache on all subsequent uses — the one-time download cost is paid at PWA install, not per compression. Target: maximum quality reduction without visible degradation. Supported formats: JPEG, PNG, HEIC, PDF. Images are decoded by the browser itself (`createImageBitmap`) and re-encoded as JPEG; a HEIC file the browser cannot decode is uploaded as-is, uncompressed, within the 10 MB limit (§20), and shown in the detail view as a download link rather than inline. PDFs are uploaded unchanged. Stored in Tigris object storage as a private object, retrieved through short-lived presigned URLs (§20). Images are displayed inline in the transaction detail view.

#### 6.14 Authentication & Profile Isolation
Email + password authentication via better-auth, with email one-time codes as the planned follow-on sign-in method (§21). Each profile is isolated from every other: `profile_members` is the sole authority for membership (§11), every tenant-scoped table carries `profile_id NOT NULL`, and all data access runs through a per-request profile-scoped accessor that verifies membership once and then injects `profile_id` into every read and write (§19). The unscoped database handle is exported from one quarantined module and blocked by lint everywhere else (§12). Cross-profile access is prevented in two layers: the accessor, and Postgres Row Level Security on every tenant-scoped table as a database backstop — a request-path query that skips the accessor returns no rows rather than another tenant's (§19). Each tenant-scoped table ships with a mandatory integration test proving one profile cannot read, update, or delete another's rows, both through the accessor and through a raw query on the app role. The one path that bypasses RLS is the owner-role handle used for migrations and profile bootstrap, quarantined by §12.

#### 6.15 Internationalisation (i18n)
The UI is built on an English string base. Estonian is layered on top as the first translation. The translation architecture must support adding further languages (e.g. Finnish, Latvian) by adding a new locale file without touching application code. Locale governs UI language only. Date format (DD.MM.YYYY) and number format (Estonian rules: decimal comma, space grouping) are fixed app-wide and do not follow the locale; amount input accepts either `.` or `,` as the decimal mark (§16). Currency is **not** locale-driven — it is a per-profile property set at creation (§10) and rendered via `Intl.NumberFormat` using the profile's `currency_code`.

#### 6.16 PWA
The app is a Progressive Web App. It is installable from the browser on Android and iOS (add to home screen). A service worker caches the app shell, static assets, and the jSquash MozJPEG WASM binary (§23a) on install. The app loads instantly on repeat visits from cache. If the user loses network connection mid-session, a visible banner informs them that they are offline and that any transaction they attempt to submit will not be saved until connection is restored. Partially filled forms are preserved in local state so no input is lost when connection returns. No native app store distribution required for MVP.

#### 6.17 Opening Balance
A party can be given a starting balance — for books that begin partway through a party's real history (e.g. a bank account that already holds €500, or a loan the company already owes €1,000 on) — via a dedicated "Set Opening Balance" action, available from party creation or later from the party's detail view. The user enters an amount and picks whether it's a credit or debit starting position; the app posts a single Record against the system `Opening Balances` party (§6.2) in the corresponding direction, inside its own TransactionGroup (`type = 'opening_balance'`) so it is visible in transaction history like any other transaction. *(Unlike legacy's equivalent, which wrote a single-sided orphan Record with no TransactionGroup, violating the same double-entry invariant §9 defines — see `business-logic-spec.md` §5.2.)* Not restricted to party creation — can be used later to true up a party's balance without a full historical record backfill. Repeatable per party; no single "the" opening balance is tracked or locked after the fact. See §25.

#### 6.18 Invoices & Payments
Received and Issued Invoices can be **extended** with their payments, as in legacy — but each payment stays its own TransactionGroup in the database (§26) and only the display groups them.

- **Record payment.** An invoice's detail view has a "Record payment" action. It opens an Expense form (Received Invoice) or an Income form (Issued Invoice) pre-filled with the supplier or customer, the amount still owed, today's date, and the description "Invoice paid: <invoice description>" (translated). The money-source choice of §6.3 / §6.4 applies as usual. The invoice's number and attachment are shown on the form for reference, read-only. On save, the payment is linked to the invoice. This is the only way to link a payment in MVP; an existing Expense or Income cannot be attached afterwards.
- **Invoice fields.** Both invoice types gain two optional fields: **invoice number** (free text — the supplier's number or the user's own; no automatic numbering) and **due date**. The app records invoices made elsewhere; it does not generate invoice documents.
- **Payment status**, computed when shown and never stored: **Unpaid** (no payments), **Partly paid · 30,00 € left**, or **Paid**. An unpaid or partly paid invoice past its due date also shows **Overdue**.
- **Grouped display.** In the transaction list's "All" view, an invoice and its payments form **one expandable row**: the invoice row shows party, invoice amount (unsigned, with the "Invoice" badge per §6.10) and status; expanding it lists each payment with its date, money source, and signed amount. The group sits in the list at the date of its latest activity (the invoice date or its most recent payment), so recording a payment brings the invoice back to the top. The "Money in" / "Money out" filters show payments as ordinary rows with a link to their invoice, so those views still add up to cash flow. A date-range filter includes a group when any of its members falls in the range. The party balance view (§6.12) is unaffected — it lists Records.
- **Open invoices.** A filter showing unpaid and partly paid invoices, split into **To pay** (received) and **To collect** (issued), each with a total of the amounts still owed, sorted by due date.
- **Overpayment is refused.** A payment above the amount still owed fails validation ("This is more than the 30,00 € still owed"). A genuine overpayment is recorded as a separate Income or Expense.
- **Protection.** An invoice with linked payments cannot be deleted until its payments are deleted, and its amount cannot be lowered below the total already paid. Deleting a payment is always allowed and updates the invoice's status immediately.

#### 6.19 Cash Custody & Settlement
Separates "cash this person is holding for the company" from "money the company owes this person", which legacy mixed into a single Partner balance (`cash-custodian.md`). See §31.

- **Custodian link.** A `CashRegister` party can optionally name a custodian — a Partner or Worker party. "Cash held by John" is a register whose custodian is John; a register without one is an unattended drawer (e.g. office petty cash). The custodian's own party balance then reflects only true receivables/payables (loans, reimbursements), never cash custody.
- **Withdrawal** moves money Bank → CashRegister (§6.7), creating "Cash held by <name>" on the fly when needed.
- **Settlement** — the custodian returns unspent cash. The user picks the source register and the destination bank; the amount is pre-filled with the register's current balance and can be changed. One Record: CashRegister → Bank.
- **Spending custody cash** is an ordinary Expense paid from that register (§6.3).
- **Closing a register** — deactivate (soft-delete, §8) once its balance is zero; the app refuses while cash is still held.
- **Party list** groups CashRegister parties by custodian, e.g. "John holds 200,00 € · Office cash 150,00 €".

#### 6.20 Members & Invitations
A profile can have several members, each with one role. See §32 for the data model.

| | owner | accountant | worker |
|---|---|---|---|
| See all transactions and balances | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ only their own entries and their own cash register |
| Create transactions | ✅ all 9 types | ✅ all 9 types | Expense only, paid from their own register or personally |
| Edit / delete transactions | ✅ any | ✅ only ones they created | ✅ only their own, until an owner or accountant has edited it |
| Manage parties | ✅ incl. deactivate | ✅ create and edit | ❌ |
| Invite / remove members, profile settings | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ |

- **Invitations.** The owner enters an email address and a role; the app emails a link (§22). If the invitee has no account, they sign up through the link and join on verification. Invitations expire after 7 days and can be revoked before they're accepted.
- **Party link.** Accepting an invitation creates, or lets the owner pick, the member's Partner or Worker party. That link pre-fills "me" as the partner on forms, scopes a worker's view to their own entries, and makes "Cash held by <name>" registers (§6.19) resolve their custodian.
- **Ownership.** Exactly one owner per profile. The owner can transfer ownership to another member, becoming an accountant.
- **Leaving.** The owner can remove any other member; any non-owner can leave. Removing a member keeps their party and every transaction they recorded; only the membership goes.

## 7. Product Principles

**Double-entry under the hood, single-entry in the UI.** The user sees "I paid 150 € to Telia". The engine records `Credit: Bank, Debit: Expenses, 150 €`. The accounting correctness is never the user's problem.

**Mobile-first, not mobile-only.** Primary design target is a 390 px viewport. Desktop is supported but not the design driver.

**Zero setup for the happy path.** System parties are pre-seeded on profile creation. The user records their first transaction without configuring anything.

**Profiles are cheap.** Creating a new profile for a new business, project, or household context costs one tap. No plan upgrades, no limits.

**English base, any language on top.** All strings are externalised. Estonian ships with v1. Adding a new language is a translation file PR, nothing more.

**Open and self-hostable.** The hosted version (Fly.io) and the self-hosted version are the same codebase — in fact the same Docker image, differing only in environment variables (§17). No features gated behind the hosted version.

## 8. Technical Constraints

- **Frontend:** Next.js, deployed as a Docker image on Fly.io Machines (`next start`). PWA via a service-worker integration — `next-pwa` is unmaintained, so the toolchain is named in §23 rather than here. Target < 200 KB initial JS per route, measured **gzip as served by `next start`** and enforced by `.size-limit.js` in CI (§29). *(Revised 2026-11-03: the unit was previously unstated; Next.js and React alone are ~450 KB raw, so only the compressed reading is achievable.)*
- **Backend / Server-side logic:** Next.js Node runtime. Server Actions for in-app mutations (transactions, parties, settings); Route Handlers (`app/api/...`) for webhooks, external integrations, and file streaming (e.g. CSV export). Database credentials, object-storage keys, and the better-auth secret are held only in the Next.js Node environment (Fly secrets) and never reach the browser.
- **Database:** Fly.io Managed Postgres — plain PostgreSQL, no vendor-specific extensions. Accessed through Prisma Client over a `pg` connection pool (`@prisma/adapter-pg`); schema and migrations by Prisma (§5, §18). Tenant isolation: profile-scoped accessor in application code, with Postgres Row Level Security as a backstop (two roles — app and owner): see §19. Procedural-logic rules: see §4 (and the §4a trigger carve-out for column hygiene). Migrations and type generation: §18. Primary key strategy: §6. Audit-field convention: §7.
- **Auth:** better-auth running in the Next.js Node runtime — email + password for MVP, email one-time codes as the planned follow-on (§21). Auth tables live in the same Postgres database as application tables. OAuth providers (Google, etc.) can be added later via better-auth's social providers without architectural changes.
- **Storage:** Tigris (S3-compatible object storage on Fly.io) for document attachments — private bucket, presigned URLs only (§20). Client-side image compression before upload.
- **Email:** Transactional email (verification, password reset, later OTP codes) over SMTP on both the hosted deployment and self-host, behind a single module (§22).
- **Hosting:** Fly.io — app Machines (Docker), Managed Postgres, Tigris object storage. The app reaches Postgres over the organisation's private network; the database is not exposed to the public internet (§17).
- **Self-host path:** Docker Compose config providing the same app image + PostgreSQL + Garage (S3-compatible; MinIO was archived, replaced 2026-11-03), with SMTP for email. A static-only frontend is not viable because Server Actions and Route Handlers require a Node runtime. Hosted and self-hosted run the identical image and differ only in environment variables, so drift is structurally prevented rather than policed.
- **i18n:** Locale files in JSON or equivalent. English as the source locale. Estonian ships in the same release.
- **Runtime baseline:** Node 26 (Active LTS), Zod 4, pnpm (§14). Runtime and package decisions are recorded in §23; exact pinned versions of the whole core stack live in `stack-versions.md`.
- **License:** MIT.

## 9. Decisions Log

Stable identifiers use the `§` prefix so other sections (and future docs) can reference a decision without restating it. Once assigned, a `§N` is never reused — superseded entries are kept in place and marked, not renumbered.

| § | Question | Decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| §1 | Offline behaviour | Read-only offline. App shows a persistent offline banner; form submissions are blocked with a clear message; form state is preserved in memory so no input is lost when connection returns. | Read-only offline means nothing is written while disconnected, so there is nothing to sync and no conflicts to resolve — which matters more now that profiles can have several members (§32), where offline writes would need real conflict handling. Simplicity wins; offline entry with sync stays v2. |
| §2 | Frontend framework | Next.js | Larger ecosystem, easier to onboard contributors. *(The original rationale also cited first-class Vercel integration; that no longer applies since §17 moved hosting to Fly.io. The choice stands on the remaining grounds — Next.js runs as a plain Node server, which is what made the move possible at all.)* |
| §3 | Image compression | MozJPEG via WASM — originally Squoosh, now jSquash (§23a) | WASM binary is cached by the service worker at install time — the 1–2 MB cost is paid once, never again. Best quality-to-size ratio of the three options. **Package settled 2026-10-31:** the decision itself — WASM, MozJPEG, service-worker-cached — is unchanged; the unmaintained Squoosh libraries are replaced by **jSquash** (`@jsquash/jpeg`, the same MozJPEG encoder repackaged), with HEIC handled by the browser's native decoder and an uncompressed fallback — see §23(a). |
| §4 | Server-side logic location | All procedural logic runs on the Next.js Node runtime (Server Actions + Route Handlers). Postgres stored procedures / RPC / PL-pgSQL functions are forbidden, as is any second server-side runtime for business logic (originally Supabase Edge Functions; now equally a separate Fly Machine, worker service, or database-side function). DB triggers are forbidden except under the §4a carve-out. **Revised 2026-08-05 (Supabase → Fly.io):** the database is plain PostgreSQL used as storage only — see §18. | Avoid lock-in and keep the dependency surface minimal — one runtime, one language, one place to look for business logic. Edge Functions (Deno) and DB-side procedures (PL/pgSQL) would each add a second execution environment with its own deploy pipeline, debugging story, and migration path away from Supabase. |
| §4a | Trigger carve-out for column hygiene | A `BEFORE` trigger may set a column on the same row **only** when (1) the value is a constant or a pure function of that row's own values, and (2) the trigger guards on `NEW IS DISTINCT FROM OLD` so no-op UPDATEs don't fire it. Permitted: `updated_at = now()`, denormalised counters/totals computed solely from this row. Forbidden: cross-table reads or writes, validation, cascades, calls into other functions, any side effect. | The original intent of §4 was to avoid Supabase-specific lock-in (Edge Functions, Realtime, Webhooks) and to keep business logic in one place. A vanilla-Postgres trigger that maintains `updated_at` violates neither — it migrates with the schema to any Postgres host and isn't business logic. The `IS DISTINCT FROM` guard means `WHERE updated_at > $cursor` (sync, incremental queries, cache invalidation) only returns rows that genuinely changed. |
| §5 | Schema migration tool | **Revised 2026-11-04: Prisma ORM** — `prisma/schema.prisma` declares tables, columns, relations and indexes; **Prisma Migrate** generates the SQL migrations in `prisma/migrations/`; **Prisma Client** runs every query. What the schema language cannot express (RLS policies, triggers, CHECK constraints, grants) is hand-written SQL added to the generated migration. Full toolchain in §18. (Superseded 2026-11-04: dbmate + hand-written SQL migrations, no ORM, kysely-codegen types, Kysely queries. Superseded 2026-08-05: Supabase CLI + `supabase gen types`.) | Logic is built up from user stories in an agent-driven workflow; the simplest, most explicit option wins. Prisma is the most widely known TypeScript data layer, so one schema file, generated migrations and a generated client give the shortest path from a story to a working table, for a human or an agent. The 2026-08-05 objection, that `schema.prisma` becomes a second source of truth beside the SQL, is answered by a split rather than ignored: the schema file owns what it can express, the migration owns only what it cannot, and CI proves the two agree (§29). |
| §6 | Primary key strategy | UUIDv7, generated in Node by Prisma Client (`@default(uuid(7))`; revised 2026-11-04, previously the `uuidv7` npm package). Every persistent table uses `id uuid PRIMARY KEY` (`@id @db.Uuid`). Vendor tables keep their library defaults (better-auth, §21). | Globally unique, time-sortable so B-tree index locality holds, ready for offline writes in v2, and parent IDs can be minted before insert so multi-row transactions don't need `RETURNING id` round-trips. Prisma mints the id in the client, not as a database default, so the second and third properties still hold. |
| §7 | Audit fields | Every persistent table has `created_at timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()`, `updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()`, `created_by_user_id uuid NULL`, `updated_by_user_id uuid NULL`. The user-id columns are plain `uuid` with **no** FK to better-auth's `user` table. Server Actions populate `created_by_user_id` / `updated_by_user_id` from the session. System-seeded rows leave `created_by_user_id` null. `updated_at` is maintained by a single shared `BEFORE UPDATE` trigger per §4a, advancing only when `NEW IS DISTINCT FROM OLD`. | Adding the four columns now is cheap; retrofitting `updated_by_user_id` once `accountant` / `worker` roles ship is painful. No FK to better-auth's `user` table because that schema is owned by better-auth's generator (§21) and a hard FK would either block user deletion or cascade it into financial history; a soft reference is sufficient. The trigger-driven `updated_at` removes per-Server-Action boilerplate without re-introducing business logic in the database. |
| §8 | Soft- vs hard-delete | Split by entity. **Soft-delete** (column `deleted_at timestamptz NULL`, default `NULL`) for `profile` and `party` — Records reference parties, so hard-deletion would break historical balance views; profile loss would be unrecoverable. **Hard-delete** with `ON DELETE CASCADE` for `transaction_group` → `record` — if the user removes a transaction it's gone, and a soft-deleted Record would force an `IS NULL` filter into every balance recalculation and every double-entry sum check. `deleted_at` participates in §4a's `IS DISTINCT FROM` guard like any other column, so soft-deleting a row bumps `updated_at`. RLS policies filter `deleted_at IS NULL` for write paths and pickers; balance views deliberately ignore the filter. | Mixing conventions costs a small amount of "remember which entity is which", but the alternatives are worse: soft-delete-everywhere pollutes every aggregate and one missed filter silently breaks the books, hard-delete-everywhere destroys Party history. The chosen split keeps the bookkeeping engine's invariants simple while preserving the references that historical views depend on. Preserving full record history (separate archive table / JSONB tombstone) is deferred — see backlog "Deleted-record preservation". |
| §9 | Money type | Integer minor units stored as `amount_minor bigint NOT NULL`. The currency's "minor units per major" (EUR=2dp, JPY=0dp, KWD=3dp, …) is a property of the *currency*, not of the column — so a single column type serves every currency without a schema migration. TypeScript carries values as `bigint`; a `Money` value object `{ amountMinor: bigint, currency: string }` is the only place majors and minors are bridged. Zod schemas at the Server Action boundary (§3 — to be defined) refuse non-integers, negatives, and values ≥ 2^53. Display formatting (`Intl.NumberFormat`) and the amount-input parser (§16) are the only places majors appear in code. FX rates, when they land in v2+, get their own `numeric(20,10)` column on a separate `fx_rate` table — rates and amounts do not share a representation. | The double-entry zero-sum invariant (sum of credit/debit Records in a TransactionGroup = 0) must be exact in every language and every transport, including JSON. Float drift in JS `number` (e.g. `10.10 + 10.20 - 20.30 ≠ 0`) makes `numeric` columns unsafe the moment a value is parsed to `Number` anywhere in the stack — a discipline the codebase loses over time. `bigint` integer arithmetic is exact natively, so the invariant holds without a Decimal library on the client. The `amount_minor` column name makes "this is in cents/yen/fils" visible at every read site instead of relying on developers remembering the convention. |
| §10 | Currency scope | Single currency per profile for MVP. `currency_code char(3) NOT NULL` lives on **both** `profile` *and* `transaction_group` (denormalised). For MVP a `CHECK` constraint — or equivalent guard in the Server Action — enforces `transaction_group.currency_code = profile.currency_code` so every Record in a profile shares one currency. No FX tables, no conversion logic, no multi-currency balance views in MVP. Currency is set on profile creation via a picker with `EUR` pre-selected; once set it is immutable. Mixed-currency users create a second profile. v2+ multi-currency path: drop the `CHECK`, add an `fx_rate` table with `numeric(20,10)` rate column (per §9) and a rate-on-date resolver Server Action; existing single-currency profiles keep working unchanged because their `currency_code` already matches everywhere. Currency lives on `transaction_group`, **not** on `record` — Records inside one TransactionGroup must share a currency by definition (a "paid €100 to Telia" cannot have one Record in EUR and the other in USD), and putting currency on `record` would invite a class of bugs that putting it on `transaction_group` makes structurally impossible. | A multi-currency engine in MVP is non-trivial: FX rate source (manual entry vs ECB feed), rate-on-date semantics, balance views that re-convert at query time, and a UI that surfaces "your bank balance in EUR, converted from USD as of today" without confusing non-accountants. Target users in §3 are mostly EUR-only Estonian small businesses; the cost of carrying that engine through v1 is paid by every user for the benefit of a small minority. The denormalised `currency_code` on `transaction_group` is the cheap insurance: adding a `NOT NULL` column to a populated table later is a 2-step migration (add nullable → backfill → flip to NOT NULL) that locks the table during backfill — painful on a multi-tenant hosted instance. Adding it now while the table is empty is free and de-risks the v2 path. |
| §11 | Tenant isolation (membership model) | **Revised 2026-08-05 (Supabase → Fly.io/better-auth), and again 2026-10-31: the membership model below stands unchanged; enforcement is the accessor plus the RLS backstop of §19.** `profile_members(id, user_id, profile_id, role, …audit fields per §7)` is the sole source of truth for "is this user allowed to touch this profile", where `user_id` is a soft reference to better-auth's `user.id` (§21). Every tenant-scoped table — `profile`, `party`, `transaction_group`, `record`, attachment metadata, future profile-owned tables — carries `profile_id uuid NOT NULL`. Enforcement is the per-request profile-scoped accessor of §19, backed by Postgres RLS on every tenant-scoped table: it resolves the session user via better-auth, verifies an active `profile_members` row for the requested `profile_id` once, then injects that `profile_id` into every read, sets it on every insert, and strips it from every update payload so no row can be moved between tenants. `role` is `text` with `CHECK (role IN ('owner','accountant','worker'))`; Permissions per role are defined in §6.20 / §32 and enforced by the accessor. Indexes: `UNIQUE (user_id, profile_id)` (covers the membership-check lookup and prevents duplicate rows) and `INDEX (profile_id, user_id)` (covers "list members of profile X"). No standalone `INDEX (user_id)` — covered by the unique's leading column. Memberships are hard-deleted (no `deleted_at`) — soft-deleted memberships would force `IS NULL` filters into every membership check and one miss = silent cross-tenant leak. Tombstone-of-removed-members goes to `backlog.md` alongside deleted-record preservation if anyone ever asks. Active-profile selection is a frontend concern (URL segment / context state); the data layer only checks membership, never "active-ness." | A `profile_members` table carries profile sharing, which ships in MVP (§6.20) with all three roles. JWT custom claims would force a token refresh on every profile switch and bake UI state into auth state; session GUCs as the membership *authority* would require remembering to `SET` on every request, and a session-level miss uses stale state. (The transaction-local `app.user_id` / `app.profile_id` settings of §19 are not an authority — they are set in one place, by the accessor, after it has checked `profile_members`, and they vanish at transaction end.) The hazard RLS's `WITH CHECK` clause closes is closed twice — by the policy (§19) and by the accessor: `USING`-style checks only validate the *old* row, so a member could otherwise `UPDATE party SET profile_id = '<other-tenant>'` and move a row out of their own tenant. The accessor strips `profile_id` from update payloads rather than validating it, which makes that write unrepresentable instead of merely rejected. The role vocabulary in the CHECK constraint is set now because changing a CHECK constraint on a populated table is a managed migration; the accessor code and policy expressions referencing it are the cheap part to change. |
| §12 | Unscoped database access | **Revised 2026-08-05 (Supabase → Fly.io/better-auth), and 2026-10-31 (RLS backstop, §19).** Server Actions and Route Handlers use the profile-scoped accessor of §19 for ~99% of code paths. The unscoped Prisma Client — connected as the `sandbucket_owner` role, which as table owner **bypasses RLS** (§19) — is exported from a single quarantined module, deliberately named `lib/db/unsafe.ts`, and an ESLint `no-restricted-imports` rule blocks importing it from anywhere except `lib/db/` itself and a named, audited allowlist: signup seed (creating the user's first profile + `profile_members` row, before any membership exists to scope against), new-profile creation (inserting system parties alongside the membership row in one transaction), invitation acceptance (inserting the invitee's `profile_members` row and party link, before they are a member), and the migration / seed scripts that run outside a request context. GDPR erasure, admin features, and platform metrics are out of MVP scope but inherit the same quarantine when they land. Database credentials live only in the Next.js Node runtime as Fly secrets per §8 and never reach the browser. | With RLS in place (§19), the request pool can no longer leak across tenants even if a query skips the accessor — so the one remaining way to do so is this owner-role handle, and the allowlist is a review gate, not a convenience. One file, one lint rule, and an import path that reads `from '@/lib/db/unsafe'` at every call site means bypassing tenant scoping is impossible to do quietly: a reviewer sees the word `unsafe` in the diff. Per §4, `SECURITY DEFINER` bootstrap functions remain forbidden, so there is no database-side alternative for the bootstrap paths — they must run from Node, which is why the allowlist exists at all rather than being empty. |
| §13 | Validation contract | Zod is the single source of truth at every trust boundary. Schemas live in `lib/schemas/*.ts`, colocated by domain (`party.ts`, `transaction.ts`, `profile.ts`, …), and are imported by **both** the client (react-hook-form via `@hookform/resolvers/zod`) and Server Actions / Route Handlers (`schema.parse(input)` on entry). The client copy is UX-only — Server Actions re-parse every input on entry and never trust that the client validated. Three distinct schema flavours per domain, kept separate: (a) **input schemas** — what the user submits, e.g. `CreateExpenseInput`, `UpdatePartyInput`; these are the only schemas imported by forms; (b) **value-object schemas** — reusable building blocks like `MoneySchema`, `Iso4217Schema`, `Uuidv7Schema`, `TransactionTypeSchema`; (c) **DB row types** — *not* Zod; these are generated by `prisma generate` from `schema.prisma` per §18 and are pure TS types, never parsed at runtime. Money handling: `MoneySchema` accepts a string from form input (e.g. `"150,00"` / `"150.00"`), strips locale separators, and `.transform()`s to `{ amountMinor: bigint, currency: string }` (§9). Negatives, non-integers, and values ≥ 2^53 are rejected here, so §9's invariant is enforced once at the boundary instead of repeated in every handler. The nine transaction types (§4) are modelled as a `z.discriminatedUnion('type', [...])` so each variant declares its own required fields (a micro-investment requires `investor_party_id` + `investment_party_id`; an expense doesn't) and the parser rejects malformed shapes at entry rather than deep in the engine. Server Actions return a typed `Result<T> = { ok: true, data: T } \| { ok: false, fieldErrors: Record<string, string[]>, formError?: string }` rather than throwing — Next.js turns thrown Server Action errors into opaque 500s, and the typed shape maps cleanly onto react-hook-form's `setError(field, …)` so the same validation message that Zod produced on the client also surfaces under the same field if it only fails on the server (e.g. the cross-row `transaction_group.currency_code = profile.currency_code` check from §10). File uploads use `z.instanceof(File)` with `.refine()` for MIME type (§6.13: JPEG / PNG / HEIC / PDF) and max size (§8 default 10 MB). DB reads are not parsed — the Prisma-generated types are the trust boundary on that side, with the profile-scoped accessor (§19), CHECK constraints, and migrations (§18) as the guard. | An agent-driven build (§5) means a single user story translates into a schema change, a form change, and a Server Action change in one pass; one Zod definition imported in two places makes that a single edit rather than a coordinate-two-files-and-pray edit. Re-parsing on the server is non-negotiable — the client can be patched, replayed, or replaced (Server Actions are reachable via direct HTTP POST), and §11's RLS only checks tenant membership, not field shape. Splitting input schemas from DB row types prevents the common Zod-everywhere anti-pattern where `select * from party` is parsed against a 14-field schema on every read for no gain — `schema.prisma` *is* the schema, `prisma generate` mirrors it into TS, and runtime parsing of DB rows only adds CPU and a place for type drift to hide. The discriminated union on transaction type is the single biggest validation lever in the app: without it, the engine has to runtime-branch on `type` and re-check each variant's required fields itself, and "I forgot to validate that field" becomes a class of bug; with it, the parser refuses the request before the engine sees it. Typed `Result` rather than thrown errors is what lets the same validation UI work whether the error came from the client schema (synchronous, instant) or the server schema / cross-row invariant (after submit) — both paths produce the same `fieldErrors` shape, so the form's error rendering doesn't care which side caught it. |

| §16 | i18n library and locale source | **next-intl**, with the active locale carried in a cookie named `NEXT_LOCALE` (next-intl's default). Roll-your-own collapses the moment Estonian plural rules need implementing and offers no static-typing for message keys. **Why cookie over URL prefix.** Sandbucket is a sign-in-only SaaS — no public marketing pages with SEO concerns, no anonymous deep-link sharing where locale-in-URL would matter (the recipient can't see the page without an account anyway, and once they're signed in their own cookie governs). URL prefixes would force every `<Link href={...}>` in the codebase to be locale-aware (or to use next-intl's locale-prefixing Link wrapper, which is itself a tax to remember), double the conceptual sitemap, and add noise to every URL a user ever copies into a support ticket. The cookie route eliminates that tax for zero functional loss in this product. **Why fall back to `Accept-Language` on first visit rather than always defaulting to English.** An Estonian-speaking user opening the app for the first time should see Estonian without having to find a settings toggle — the §3 primary persona is Estonian. The browser already knows the user's language preference; respecting it is the lowest-friction default and Next.js middleware does it in two lines. The English fallback only kicks in when the browser advertises no supported language. **Why no profile-level locale for MVP.** A `profile.preferred_locale` column would require schema, RLS, a Server Action to read/write it, and reconciliation logic when the cookie and profile disagree (cookie wins on this device, but on a new device which wins?). For MVP the cookie alone is a clean single-source truth: per-device, per-browser, no schema, no Server Action, no reconciliation. If users ask for cross-device sync later, the v2 path is a single column plus a sign-in hook that writes the column value into the cookie; existing cookies keep working unchanged. **Why the library handles *strings only*.** Decoupling strings from numbers / dates / currency keeps each concern in one place: next-intl owns translation, the `Intl.*` APIs own formatting, and the profile owns currency (§10). Bundling currency formatting under the i18n library — which most libraries try to do via their own number-formatting helpers — would re-introduce the locale-as-currency coupling that §10 explicitly rejected (Estonian-locale users with USD profiles would silently render dollars as euros under a naive integration), and would mean shipping a second implementation of `Intl.NumberFormat` that the runtime already provides for free. **Why Estonian number rules in both locales (2026-11-02).** The audience is Estonian businesses (§3) and the English UI is read by the same people, so one number format avoids two readings of the same amount. Input accepts `.` too because a phone's decimal keypad shows only its own OS locale's mark — an English-set phone often offers only `.`; with grouping never typed as `.` or `,`, the single mark is unambiguous. **Why DD.MM.YYYY for both locales.** Estonian users expect it (CLDR Estonian default), and the English-speaking subset of the §3 audience is overwhelmingly Estonian small-business owners reading English UI — DD.MM.YYYY is unambiguous for them. A US-English MM/DD/YYYY default would be wrong for half the audience and confusingly American for the other half. ISO 8601 (YYYY-MM-DD) would be unambiguous globally but reads as machine output, not friendly. The PRD §6.15 default sticks. |

| §19 | Tenant isolation enforcement (accessor + RLS backstop) | **Revised 2026-10-31: Postgres Row Level Security is reinstated as a database backstop behind the accessor, superseding the 2026-08-05 "no RLS" version of this entry. The membership model in §11 is unchanged.** Isolation has two layers. **(1) Profile-scoped accessor — the primary path.** `forProfile(profileId)` resolves the better-auth session, opens a transaction, sets `app.user_id` from the session with `set_config('app.user_id', <id>, true)`, verifies exactly once that a `profile_members` row exists for `(session.user.id, profileId)`, then sets `app.profile_id` the same way. Both settings are **transaction-local** (the `true` argument — equivalent to `SET LOCAL`) and never come from client input. Thereafter the accessor injects `profile_id = <active>` into every read, sets it on every insert, and strips it from every update payload so a row cannot be moved between tenants. All feature code depends on the accessor; the owner-role handle is quarantined per §12. The explicit `profile_id` predicate stays even though RLS would filter anyway: it keeps query plans on the `profile_id` indexes and keeps intent visible in the query. **(2) RLS backstop — the database.** Every tenant-scoped data table (`party`, `transaction_group`, `record`, attachment metadata, future profile-owned tables) ships, in the same migration that creates it, with `ALTER TABLE <t> ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` and one policy: `CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON <t> USING (profile_id = current_setting('app.profile_id', true)::uuid) WITH CHECK (profile_id = current_setting('app.profile_id', true)::uuid)`. The policy checks the **profile only (Option A)**; membership is verified by the accessor, not re-checked per row. Two tables are keyed by user instead, because they must be read *before* or *across* an active profile (the membership check itself, and the profile switcher): `profile_members` — `USING (user_id = current_setting('app.user_id', true)::uuid OR profile_id = current_setting('app.profile_id', true)::uuid) WITH CHECK (profile_id = current_setting('app.profile_id', true)::uuid)`, a user sees their own memberships everywhere plus the other members of the active profile (§6.20); `profile` — `USING (id = current_setting('app.profile_id', true)::uuid OR EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM profile_members m WHERE m.profile_id = profile.id AND m.user_id = current_setting('app.user_id', true)::uuid))`, a user sees the profiles they belong to. RLS has exactly one job — stop another tenant's rows being returned or written; every query and all business logic stay in the Node runtime per §4. **Roles.** Two Postgres roles. `sandbucket_app` serves all request traffic: it does not own the tables, so RLS applies to it, and it is granted DML only (no DDL, no `BYPASSRLS`). `sandbucket_owner` owns the schema, runs Prisma migrations (§18), and backs the `lib/db/unsafe.ts` handle (§12); as table owner it bypasses RLS, which is why that handle stays quarantined. Two connection strings, both Fly secrets (§8); the request pool uses `sandbucket_app`. `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` is deliberately not set — it would subject the owner role too and break the bootstrap paths that legitimately run before any membership exists. **Tests.** Every tenant-scoped table ships with an integration test that seeds two profiles and asserts profile B cannot read, update, or delete profile A's rows (a) through the accessor and (b) through a raw unfiltered query on `sandbucket_app` with B's settings applied — (b) proves the policy exists and is correct, independently of the accessor. A shared test asserts that with no settings applied `sandbucket_app` sees zero rows in every tenant-scoped table. A new tenant-scoped table without its policy or these tests is a review blocker. Attachment authorisation follows the same path: presigned URLs (§20) are minted only after the accessor has read the attachment row, which RLS also guards. | The 2026-08-05 version dropped RLS because a pooled connection still carrying a previous request's setting could serve the wrong tenant. That hazard belongs to session-level `SET`. With transaction-local settings the value is discarded at `COMMIT`/`ROLLBACK`, so nothing survives into the next request on the same pooled connection; and `current_setting(..., true)` returns `NULL` when unset, which matches no rows. A forgotten setting therefore fails **closed and loud** — the query returns nothing and the first test catches it — rather than silently returning another tenant's data. With that hazard closed, RLS is cheap: two lines per table, written alongside the table; one transaction per request; one extra role. Adding it now rather than at v2 avoids retro-auditing every query written in between, and v2 multi-user sharing widens the blast radius of any leak. The accessor alone left isolation dependent on every query being written correctly; the backstop makes a query that forgot its filter, an ad-hoc report, or a future script return nothing instead of leaking. **Why Option A (profile-only policy) over a membership-checking policy on every table:** a policy joining `profile_members` would verify membership in the database too, but every row read on every data table would pay a correlated subquery, and the policies would start encoding role logic that changes in v2. The `profile` table's `EXISTS` is the one exception, confined to a small table read on profile switch. **Costs accepted:** each request holds one pooled connection for the duration of its transaction (immaterial at MVP scale); migrations must remember the policy, which the zero-rows test and review rule catch. **Not a §4 violation:** RLS policies are declarative row filters — no PL/pgSQL, no triggers, no `SECURITY DEFINER`. |

Number formatting follows the active locale via `Intl.NumberFormat` (§16). **Spacing and shape.** Tailwind's default 4 px spacing scale; `--radius` 0.5 rem (8 px); pill shape for chips and segmented filters. **Mobile-first rules (binding on every screen).** Every interactive element is at least 44 × 44 px; primary actions sit in the bottom third within thumb reach (bottom navigation with a centred add button); forms open as bottom `<Sheet>`s on mobile (§15); amount fields use `inputmode="decimal"`; receipt inputs accept camera capture; fixed bars respect `env(safe-area-inset-*)`; colour never carries meaning on its own. | **Zinc** is the most neutral of the three grey families, so financial data reads calmly and the blue primary sits on it without a colour cast; Slate's blue tint would have doubled up on the primary, and Stone's warm grey goes muddy in dark mode. **Blue** reads as trustworthy and bank-like and, unlike teal or green, does not compete with the green used for money coming in — the one semantic colour in this app that appears on nearly every screen. Near-black (shadcn's default) was the low-commitment alternative; blue was chosen to give the app an identity now, and because the tokens are variables it remains a one-line swap if a brand colour arrives later. **System font** costs zero bytes against the §8 200 KB budget, renders instantly offline (§1), and makes the installed PWA (§6.16) feel native on each platform; the system stacks on iOS and Android both have good tabular digits. Inter / Geist would have bought cross-device consistency at ~20–30 KB, which isn't worth it for a utility app. **Sign + colour** was chosen over sign-only (slower to scan on a phone) and In/Out words (busier, and one more string to translate per §6.15). |
| §28 | Testing plan | **Decided 2026-10-31.** Three layers, all run on every pull request. **(1) Unit — Vitest.** The double-entry engine, the `Money` value object (§9), and the Zod schemas (§13). | §29 | CI/CD | **Decided 2026-10-31. GitHub Actions; merging to `main` deploys straight to production.** **On every pull request:** lint, typecheck, the three test layers of §28, a Prisma drift check (`prisma migrate diff` from the committed migrations to `schema.prisma` must be empty, §18), and a `size-limit` check that fails the PR if initial JS exceeds 200 KB (§8). All must pass to merge. **On merge to `main`:** `fly deploy` from GitHub Actions. Migrations run as Fly's **`release_command`** (`prisma migrate deploy`, with the Prisma CLI shipped in the image and connecting as `sandbucket_owner`, §19): it runs in a temporary Machine on the new image before any app Machine is replaced, and a failing migration aborts the deploy, leaving the previous version serving. CI itself never holds database credentials. **Migration rule (expand / contract):** because the previous version is still serving while `release_command` runs, every migration must be compatible with the code already in production — e.g. add a column in one release and drop the old one in a later release; never rename or drop in the same release that stops using it. **No preview environments and no staging for MVP.** **On a version tag (`v*`):** the same Docker image is published to GitHub Container Registry for self-hosters (§7), who apply migrations with the same `prisma migrate deploy` via the `migrate` service in `compose.yaml`. | Running migrations as `release_command` instead of a separate CI job that proxies into Managed Postgres keeps database credentials out of GitHub entirely and ties the migration to the exact image that will run it. The expand / contract rule is the price of that ordering, and it's the same discipline zero-downtime deploys need anyway. Per-PR previews (B) and a staging app (C) were both rejected for MVP: with real Postgres integration tests and end-to-end tests against the production image on every PR (§28), the remaining risk doesn't justify a second app and database or per-PR throwaway databases for a solo project. **Revisit C (staging + tag-promoted production) once real users depend on the hosted service.** Publishing the self-host image only on tags, not every merge, gives self-hosters versioned releases rather than a moving target. |
| §30 | Observability | **Decided 2026-10-31: Fly's built-in logs and metrics only — no error-tracking service (no Sentry, no GlitchTip) and no log drain for MVP.** The server writes **structured JSON logs to stdout** (one line per event: level, timestamp, request id, route, outcome, duration); unhandled errors in Server Actions and Route Handlers are logged at `error` with their stack trace and request id. Hosted: read through `fly logs` and Fly's managed Grafana metrics (Machine CPU / memory / HTTP status). Self-host: the same stdout via `docker compose logs` — identical code path, nothing to configure. **Log hygiene:** never log request bodies, amounts, party names, descriptions, attachment contents, or email addresses; identify users and profiles only by their UUIDs. **Accepted gap:** errors that happen only in the browser are not captured. | Zero extra services, accounts, cost, or third-party data processors — which also keeps the self-host story (§7) identical to hosted, and avoids a GDPR processor agreement for an error tracker. The trade-off is explicit: server errors must be found by searching logs rather than arriving as alerts, and client-side errors go unseen unless a user reports them. Acceptable for an MVP with few users and end-to-end coverage on every PR (§28). **Revisit when the hosted version has real users**; adding Sentry (EU region, opt-in by DSN) or GlitchTip later is additive and changes no application code beyond SDK initialisation. The no-sensitive-data logging rule applies regardless, because financial data must not leak into log storage that has weaker access control than the database. |
